I’m a California Mechanic Using My Own Tools. Does My Employer Have to Pay Me More?

Most shops expect you to show up with your own tool box. That expectation comes with a legal price tag your employer may owe you.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 5 min read

TL;DR

If your shop requires you to buy and bring your own tools and you don't earn much above minimum wage, California law likely requires the shop to reimburse you for that cost, separate from any wage or overtime issue.

Walk into almost any repair shop in California, whether it's a dealership in Sacramento, an independent garage in San Bernardino, or a specialty shop in San Diego, and you'll find the same unwritten rule: technicians bring their own tools. Nobody hands a new hire a box of sockets on day one. You're expected to already have thousands of dollars of equipment before you turn your first wrench.

What a lot of techs don't realize is that this arrangement isn't just an industry norm, it's something California law specifically addresses. If your shop requires tools you had to buy yourself, there's a real chance you're owed money for them.

Share this article:
01

The Law Behind Tool Reimbursement

California requires employers to reimburse employees for necessary expenses incurred while doing their job. On top of that, the state's wage orders specifically call out tools: if you earn less than twice the applicable minimum wage, your employer generally has to provide or pay for the tools and equipment the job requires.

This isn't some obscure technicality. It's a deliberate protection built for exactly this situation, industries where workers are expected to self-equip before they can even start earning.

Example

Your shop's job posting lists “must have own tools” as a requirement, but never mentions reimbursement anywhere in your offer letter or handbook.

02

Who Actually Qualifies

The two-times-minimum-wage line is the key threshold. Many flat-rate and flag-hour technicians, especially at high-volume shops in places like Fountain Valley or Beverly Hills, end up under that line during slower weeks even if their posted rate looks higher on paper.

What matters isn't your job title, it's what you actually earned per hour worked once you average out a pay period. If that number sits below twice minimum wage, the tool reimbursement requirement generally applies to you.

Example

You're paid $22 an hour on flag hours, but a few slow weeks bring your effective hourly average closer to $19. If minimum wage in your area is over $16, that's below the line.

03

What Counts as a Necessary Tool

Generally, anything the shop requires you to have in order to perform the job counts, hand tools, sockets, specialty diagnostic equipment, even a rolling tool box if the shop mandates a specific setup. Tools you buy purely out of personal preference, beyond what's actually required, are a grayer area.

Keep receipts. If you're ever in a position to raise this, being able to show what you spent and when makes the conversation far more concrete than an estimate.

Example

Your shop requires a specific scan tool brand to work on newer vehicles. That's a required tool, not an optional upgrade.

04

How Much You Might Be Owed

Tool reimbursement claims can add up quickly once you look at years of purchases rather than a single set. A career tool investment for a working technician often runs into the tens of thousands of dollars over time, and California's reimbursement rule doesn't expire just because you bought the tools a while ago.

Example

Between your original tool box, a scan tool, and ongoing replacements over three years, you've spent close to $8,000 out of pocket with no reimbursement.

05

Paying a Helper Out of Your Own Pocket

Some shops lean on an informal arrangement where a flat-rate or flag-hour technician pays a helper or apprentice directly, out of their own earnings, to assist with heavy jobs, parts runs, or cleanup between rows. It's common across shops in San Bernardino, Fountain Valley, and Beverly Hills, and it's legally shakier than most technicians realize.

Under California law, that helper is doing work that benefits the shop, often under the shop's roof and direction, which generally makes them the shop's employee, not the technician's, regardless of who physically hands over the cash. A shop can't outsource its own minimum wage, payroll tax, and workers' compensation obligations onto a technician who has no authority to actually hire anyone. If that helper gets hurt on the job, the shop's workers' comp coverage should apply, but an off-books arrangement can make that coverage murky exactly when it matters most.

There's also a wage problem for the technician. If a shop expects flat-rate pay to silently cover the cost of a helper the shop itself should be staffing and paying, that's functionally shifting a business expense onto the employee, the same kind of problem Labor Code Section 2802 is meant to prevent for tools and equipment. A technician shouldn't have to subsidize the shop's staffing decisions out of pay that's supposed to be theirs.

06

What to Do Next

Start pulling together receipts, credit card statements, or anything else that shows what you've spent on required tools. Compare that against what, if anything, your shop has reimbursed. If the gap is real, it's worth having reviewed alongside your regular pay.

Tool costs rarely show up alone. If your shop pays by flag hour or flat rate, it's worth reading our breakdown of why California mechanics get underpaid and the separate question of whether piece-rate pay still has to meet minimum wage.
