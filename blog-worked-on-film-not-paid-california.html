I Worked on a Film and Haven't Been Paid. What Can I Do in California?

A wrap date isn't a deadline for getting paid, it's the point where getting paid gets harder to ignore.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

You're still owed wages for work already performed even if a production has wrapped, run out of funding, or gone silent on payroll. California's Labor Commissioner can process a wage claim against a production company regardless of its current financial state, and a late final paycheck can trigger separate waiting-time penalties on top of the wages themselves. Acting sooner rather than later matters, especially if the production entity is at risk of dissolving.

Independent and low-budget productions in particular sometimes treat payroll as the last item on a shrinking budget, paying vendors and equipment rentals first and quietly falling behind on crew wages. A wrapped shoot, a missed final paycheck, or a production office that's stopped responding to emails doesn't mean the money owed to you has disappeared, it means the process of collecting it needs to start. The informal, project-based nature of production work makes this kind of nonpayment easier to get away with than it would be in almost any other industry, precisely because workers scatter to their next job before anyone has time to organize a collective response.

1

You're Owed Wages Even After the Production Wraps

Wages are owed for work already performed, full stop, regardless of whether the production later wraps, gets shelved, or never gets distributed. A production entity, sometimes a single-purpose LLC created just for that one project, remains legally obligated to pay for labor performed during principal photography even if the film itself never sees a release. "The project didn't work out" is not a legal defense to unpaid wages.

This is worth stating plainly because it's one of the most common things productions imply, sometimes without saying it directly, when payments start slipping: that somehow the film's commercial fate is tied to whether crew get paid for work they already did. It isn't. Wages for hours already worked are owed regardless of whether the finished project ever makes a dollar, gets picked up for distribution, or is shelved indefinitely.

This is also true for deferred-payment arrangements sometimes used on independent productions, where crew agree to reduced up-front pay in exchange for a share of future profits or distribution proceeds. Even under a legitimate deferred-pay structure, the deferred portion is still a wage obligation once the triggering event occurs, and a production can't simply treat "we never got distribution" as an excuse to avoid the underlying agreement altogether if its own terms were structured as compensation for work already performed.

2

Common Reasons Film Workers Don't Get Paid

A few patterns show up repeatedly: a production that runs out of financing mid-shoot, a payroll company that was never properly funded by the production, a check that bounces, or a production office that simply stops responding once principal photography wraps. Sometimes it's outright noncompliance, wages that were simply never issued on the legally required timeline after separation, rather than a dispute over the amount owed at all.

Independent and micro-budget productions are particularly prone to this because financing is often assembled piecemeal, sometimes with a producer covering shortfalls personally in hopes that additional funding or a distribution deal materializes after the fact. When that additional money doesn't come through, crew wages are often the first obligation quietly deprioritized, precisely because crew members have less immediate leverage than a landlord, an equipment rental house, or a bonding company.

A bounced paycheck deserves particular attention because it can sometimes carry its own separate legal consequences beyond the underlying unpaid wages, depending on the circumstances. Don't assume a bounced check simply resets the clock on when you're owed payment, the original wage obligation and its timeline are generally unaffected by a payment method that failed.

3

The Labor Commissioner Wage Claim Process

California's accepts wage claims against employers, including production companies, regardless of the size or current status of the business. The process generally involves filing a claim describing the unpaid wages, a conference or hearing, and, if the claim is upheld, an order requiring payment. This process doesn't require a lawyer to initiate, though having one review your claim before filing can help make sure it's documented correctly the first time.

Because this process moves on its own timeline and can take months to resolve, it's generally best to file as soon as it's clear a wage issue isn't going to resolve informally rather than waiting to see if the production eventually catches up on its own. A claim that's clearly documented from the start, with specific dates, hours, and amounts, also tends to move more smoothly through the conference and hearing process than one assembled after the fact from memory.

An order from the Labor Commissioner in your favor is also enforceable in court if the production still doesn't pay voluntarily, which gives the process real teeth beyond simply being a formal way of asking for money. That enforcement mechanism is part of why pursuing a claim through the proper channel, rather than relying on informal follow-up emails alone, matters when a production has already shown it isn't going to pay without pressure.

4

Waiting-Time Penalties for a Late Final Paycheck

If an employer willfully fails to pay final wages on the legally required timeline (immediately for a termination, or within 72 hours in most other separations), California allows a of up to 30 days of the employee's wages, on top of the unpaid wages themselves. On a production where multiple crew members were paid late or not at all, that penalty can turn a modest wage dispute into a substantial claim.

This penalty exists specifically to discourage employers from treating a late final paycheck as a minor administrative slip with no real consequence. For a crew member owed a few thousand dollars in wages, thirty additional days of pay calculated at their regular daily rate can meaningfully increase the total amount owed, which is exactly the kind of leverage that often gets a stalled production to finally engage with a claim seriously.

"Willful" in this context doesn't require bad intent in the everyday sense, it generally just means the employer knew wages were due and didn't pay them, as opposed to a genuine, good-faith dispute over whether money was actually owed at all. A production that simply ran out of money but knew it owed the wages is still generally within the scope of this penalty.

5

When a Production Company Shuts Down or Runs Out of Money

A single-purpose production LLC that dissolves or runs out of money complicates collection but doesn't automatically eliminate it. Depending on the facts, a parent company, a studio, a completion bond, or individual principals may bear responsibility, particularly where the production entity was undercapitalized from the start or funds were diverted improperly. This is exactly the kind of situation where acting quickly, before assets disperse further, meaningfully improves the odds of actually collecting what's owed.

Completion bonds in particular exist for exactly this kind of scenario on bonded productions, providing a financial backstop when a production can't meet its obligations. Even on unbonded independent productions, tracing where financing actually came from, and whether individual producers commingled personal and production funds, can sometimes open up additional avenues for recovery beyond the dissolved production entity itself.

Filing a claim promptly also matters for a more practical reason: a dissolving LLC may still have some remaining assets or accounts receivable in the immediate aftermath of a shoot that simply won't be there months later once everything has been wound down, distributed, or written off. Wage claims filed early are often the ones best positioned to actually collect from whatever the production still has.

6

Steps to Take Right Now

Gather your call sheets, any pay stubs or check stubs you did receive, timecards, text or email communication about your pay, and a clear record of the dates and hours you worked. Written communication asking about the missing payment, even a simple email, helps establish a timeline. If the amount is significant or the production's status is uncertain, having your specific situation reviewed quickly can help identify who is actually responsible and how best to pursue it.

It's worth reaching out to other crew members who worked the same job, since a shared pattern of nonpayment across multiple people strengthens everyone's position and can help identify who the actual decision-makers behind the production were. A coordinated, well-documented approach across several affected workers is often far more effective than each person trying to chase down payment individually.

Don't assume the passage of time has erased your claim just because the production has gone quiet for months. Wage claims generally have a filing window measured in years, not weeks, which means even a shoot that wrapped a while ago is very likely still within the timeframe to pursue what you're owed.
