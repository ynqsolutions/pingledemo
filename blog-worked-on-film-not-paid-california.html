I Worked on a Film and Haven't Been Paid. What Can I Do in California?

A wrap date isn't a deadline for getting paid, it's the point where getting paid gets harder to ignore.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

You're still owed wages for work already performed even if a production has wrapped, run out of funding, or gone silent on payroll. California's Labor Commissioner can process a wage claim regardless of a production's current financial state, and a late final paycheck can trigger separate waiting-time penalties on top of the wages themselves.

Independent and low-budget productions sometimes treat payroll as the last item on a shrinking budget, paying vendors and equipment rentals first and quietly falling behind on crew wages. A wrapped shoot or a production office that's stopped responding doesn't mean the money owed to you has disappeared, it means the process of collecting it needs to start. The informal, project-based nature of production work makes this kind of nonpayment easier to get away with than in almost any other industry.

1

You're Owed Wages Even After the Production Wraps

Wages are owed for work already performed, full stop, regardless of whether the production later wraps, gets shelved, or never gets distributed. A single-purpose production LLC remains legally obligated to pay for labor performed even if the film never sees a release. "The project didn't work out" is not a defense to unpaid wages, and this holds even under deferred-payment arrangements tied to future distribution.

This is worth stating plainly because it's one of the most common things productions imply when payments start slipping, that the film's commercial fate is somehow tied to whether crew get paid. It isn't. Wages for hours already worked are owed regardless of whether the finished project ever makes a dollar.

Example

A crew member wraps a micro-budget feature and is told payment is "pending a distribution deal." Wages for hours already worked don't depend on whether the film ever sells.

2

Common Reasons Film Workers Don't Get Paid

A few patterns show up repeatedly: a production that runs out of financing mid-shoot, a payroll company that was never properly funded, a bounced check, or a production office that simply stops responding once photography wraps. Sometimes it's outright noncompliance rather than any real dispute over the amount owed, and independent productions with piecemeal financing are especially prone to this.

Don't assume a bounced check simply resets the clock on when you're owed payment either, the original wage obligation and its timeline generally aren't affected by a payment method that failed. Sometimes a producer covers shortfalls personally hoping additional funding materializes later, and when it doesn't, crew wages are often the first obligation quietly deprioritized, precisely because crew have less immediate leverage than a landlord or equipment rental house.

3

The Labor Commissioner Wage Claim Process

California's accepts wage claims against employers, including production companies, regardless of the size or current status of the business. The process generally involves filing a claim, a conference or hearing, and, if upheld, an order requiring payment that's enforceable in court. It doesn't require a lawyer to initiate, though it's best to file as soon as it's clear a wage issue won't resolve informally.

A claim that's clearly documented from the start, with specific dates, hours, and amounts, tends to move more smoothly through the conference and hearing process than one assembled after the fact from memory. An order in your favor also gives the process real teeth if a production still doesn't pay voluntarily.

4

Waiting-Time Penalties for a Late Final Paycheck

If an employer willfully fails to pay final wages on the legally required timeline, California allows a of up to 30 days of wages on top of the unpaid wages themselves. This penalty exists specifically to discourage employers from treating a late final paycheck as a minor administrative slip with no real consequence.

Example

On a production that paid several crew members late or not at all, that penalty alone can turn a modest wage dispute into a substantial claim.

"Willful" here doesn't require bad intent in the everyday sense, it generally just means the employer knew wages were due and didn't pay them, as opposed to a genuine good-faith dispute over whether money was owed at all.

5

When a Production Company Shuts Down or Runs Out of Money

A single-purpose production LLC that dissolves complicates collection but doesn't automatically eliminate it. Depending on the facts, a parent company, a studio, a completion bond, or individual principals may bear responsibility, particularly where the entity was undercapitalized from the start, which is exactly why acting quickly before assets disperse matters.

A dissolving LLC may still have some remaining assets or receivables in the immediate aftermath of a shoot that simply won't be there months later once everything has been wound down. Completion bonds exist for exactly this scenario on bonded productions, providing a financial backstop when a production can't meet its obligations.

6

Steps to Take Right Now

Gather your call sheets, any pay stubs you did receive, timecards, and written communication about your pay. Written communication asking about the missing payment, even a simple email, helps establish a timeline. If the amount is significant or the production's status is uncertain, having your situation reviewed quickly can help identify who is actually responsible and how best to pursue it.

Don't assume the passage of time has erased your claim just because the production has gone quiet for months, wage claims generally have a filing window measured in years, not weeks. Reaching out to other crew members who worked the same job can also help identify who the actual decision-makers behind the production were, and a coordinated approach across several affected workers is often more effective than chasing payment individually.
