California Employment Law - Severance Pay

Jump to TL;DR ↓

A marketing manager in San Diego is handed a folder on a Tuesday afternoon: her position is being eliminated, and inside is a severance offer worth six weeks of pay if she signs by Friday. She has no idea whether six weeks is fair, low, or generous, because nothing about severance pay is standardized. That uncertainty is exactly why this calculator, and the explanation below it, exists.

Severance pay is money an employer offers when ending someone's employment, usually in a lump sum or over a short series of payments, and it is almost always conditioned on the employee signing a severance agreement. Unlike a final paycheck for hours already worked, severance is not something California law generally requires an employer to pay at all. Understanding what typically goes into a severance calculation, and what it doesn't cover, is the first step before agreeing to any number.

Key Points

  • California does not generally require employers to offer severance pay, with narrow exceptions like Cal-WARN violations.
  • One to two weeks of pay per year of service is a commonly cited range, but there is no legal standard, so amounts vary widely.
  • A severance agreement almost always includes a release of claims, waiving your right to sue over the termination.
  • A first offer is frequently a starting point, not a final number, especially if you have leverage.
  • Severance is taxable, and it can affect unemployment benefits depending on how it's structured.

Is Severance Pay Required in California?

Generally, no. Unlike final wages for time already worked, which California Labor Code Sections 201-203 require to be paid immediately or within 72 hours depending on the circumstances, severance pay is not a wage owed for work performed. It is a separate, typically voluntary payment offered at the employer's discretion or under an existing company policy or employment contract.

The main exception involves mass layoffs. The requires covered employers with 75 or more employees to give 60 days' written notice before a mass layoff, relocation, or plant closing. If an employer skips that notice, it can be liable for back pay and benefits for the notice period, according to the California Department of Industrial Relations.

How Severance Is Typically Calculated

Because there is no statutory formula, most severance calculations follow informal, widely used conventions rather than a legal requirement. This calculator uses the same basic building blocks employers commonly rely on:

  • Weekly pay: your annual salary divided by 52 weeks.
  • Severance multiplier: a number of weeks of pay for each year you worked there, commonly one week per year, sometimes two for longer-tenured or more senior employees.
  • Years of service: your tenure at the company, sometimes rounded up to the nearest year.
  • Add-ons: unused, accrued PTO or vacation is generally owed to you separately as wages under California law, regardless of whether you sign a severance agreement, and is sometimes bundled into the same lump-sum check.

Multiply weekly pay by the severance multiplier and by years of service, then add any PTO payout, and you get a rough estimate similar to what the calculator above produces. Real offers can also factor in role seniority, department-wide layoff policies, the reason for the separation, and simple negotiation.

A severance number is a starting point for a conversation, not a fixed rule.

What This Calculator Doesn't Account For

This tool is intentionally simple, and real severance offers involve more moving parts than a formula can capture. It does not account for:

  • Taxes. Severance is taxable wages, subject to federal and state withholding along with Social Security and Medicare, so the amount you actually receive will be lower than the gross figure calculated here.
  • The value of what you're giving up. Most severance agreements include a release of claims. If the termination itself may have involved discrimination, retaliation, or another legal violation, the value of that release, and what a claim might otherwise be worth, isn't reflected in a simple weeks-of-pay formula.
  • Benefits continuation. Employer-paid COBRA premiums, extended equity vesting, or outplacement services are sometimes offered alongside cash severance and add real value that a dollar estimate alone won't show.
  • Company-specific policy or contract terms. If you have a written employment agreement or offer letter that promises a specific severance formula, that document controls, not general industry norms.

Can You Negotiate a Severance Offer?

Often, yes. A first severance offer is frequently written to leave room, especially when it comes from HR rather than from someone with final authority over the number. Leverage matters: a long tenure, a role that's hard to backfill quickly, or facts suggesting the termination itself may have violated the law can all support asking for more, additional notice pay, extended health coverage, or a longer period to decide. Our guide to negotiating a low severance offer walks through how that conversation typically goes, and our , available in our tools and templates library, gives you a starting point in writing.

What to Check Before You Sign

Before signing any severance agreement, it's worth slowing down enough to check a few things: what claims the release actually covers, whether there's a deadline and a required review period (federal law gives employees 40 and older at least 21 days to consider certain releases, and 7 days to revoke after signing, under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act), whether a non-disparagement or confidentiality clause is attached, and whether the payment structure affects unemployment eligibility. How Severance Interacts With Unemployment and Taxes

Two questions come up constantly once an offer is on the table. First, does accepting severance affect unemployment benefits? According to the California Employment Development Department, it depends on how the payment is structured. A lump-sum payment is treated differently than severance paid out in installments that resemble continued wages, so it's worth confirming directly with the EDD how a specific offer will be treated rather than assuming either way. Second, is severance taxed? Yes. The IRS treats severance pay as taxable wages, generally subject to the same withholding as a regular paycheck, which is worth factoring in before treating the calculator's estimate as a take-home number.

TL;DR

California generally doesn't require severance pay, so there's no fixed formula, but one to two weeks of pay per year of service is a common starting point. Most offers come with a release of claims attached, are often negotiable, and are taxable. Unused PTO is owed separately either way, and a first offer is rarely the final word.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is severance pay required by law in California?

Generally no. California does not have a state law requiring most employers to offer severance pay when an employee is let go. The main exception involves the California WARN Act, which can require pay in lieu of notice for larger employers conducting a mass layoff, relocation, or plant closing without the required 60 days' notice.

How many weeks of severance is normal?

There is no fixed legal standard, but one to two weeks of pay per year of service is a commonly cited range in practice, sometimes more for longer-tenured or senior employees. The actual number is set entirely by the employer's policy or what is negotiated, not by statute.

Can I negotiate a severance offer?

Often, yes. A first severance offer is frequently a starting point rather than a final number, especially if you have leverage such as a potential legal claim, a long tenure, or a role that is difficult to backfill quickly. Negotiating typically does not withdraw the original offer.

Does signing a severance agreement waive my right to sue?

Usually, yes, that is the point of the agreement from the employer's side. Most severance agreements include a release of claims, where you give up the right to sue over the termination and related issues in exchange for the payment. That is exactly why it is worth having it reviewed before signing, particularly if you believe the termination itself may have been unlawful.

Does severance pay affect unemployment benefits in California?

It can. The California Employment Development Department evaluates severance on a case-by-case basis, and a lump-sum payment is treated differently than pay continued in installments that resemble ongoing wages. It's worth checking directly with the EDD about how a specific offer will be treated before assuming it won't affect your claim.

Is severance pay taxed?

Yes. Severance pay is considered taxable wages by the IRS and California, generally subject to federal and state income tax withholding along with Social Security and Medicare, similar to a regular paycheck.
