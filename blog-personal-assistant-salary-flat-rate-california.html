Can a Personal Assistant Be Paid a Salary or Flat Rate in California?

A salary or day rate changes how you're paid, not necessarily whether you're owed overtime. Here's the difference.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 5 min read

TL;DR

Paying a personal assistant a flat salary or day rate doesn't automatically make the role exempt from overtime, both a salary threshold and a duties test have to be met. Most personal assistant duties don't independently satisfy the duties test. A salary or day rate that doesn't reflect overtime for long days may mean significant unpaid back wages.

Plenty of personal assistants are paid a flat weekly salary or a day rate rather than an hourly wage, and plenty of employers treat that as the end of the conversation: if it's a salary, no overtime is owed. That's not how California law works. Being paid a salary is one requirement of an overtime exemption, not the whole test.

1

Salary Doesn't Automatically Mean Exempt

An employer can pay anyone a fixed salary or day rate, that's just a method of payment. Whether the role is actually exempt from overtime depends on separate legal tests, and if those tests aren't met, a salaried personal assistant can still be owed overtime for every hour worked beyond 8 in a day or 40 in a week, the salary just gets converted into an hourly equivalent for that calculation.

This trips people up because it runs against a common assumption borrowed from ordinary office jobs, that salaried automatically means overtime-exempt. Plenty of legitimately exempt roles are salaried, but plenty of non-exempt roles are salaried too. The paycheck format tells you nothing about whether the job qualifies for an exemption; it's simply how the wages get distributed.

This confusion is understandable given how many other industries default to salaried pay for entry-level and mid-level roles without much scrutiny. But California's wage-and-hour rules were written specifically to prevent employers from using pay structure as a shortcut around overtime obligations, which is exactly why the law looks at actual job duties rather than accepting the label an employer chooses to apply.

Example

A personal assistant is paid a flat $800 day rate whenever she travels with her employer, regardless of whether the day runs 8 hours or 14. Because her actual duties are scheduling and errands rather than exempt-level work, that flat rate doesn't excuse the overtime owed for the extra hours, it just becomes the starting point for calculating what she's actually owed.

2

The Two Tests That Actually Matter

To qualify as exempt, a role generally has to satisfy both a (a minimum salary threshold, at least twice the state minimum wage for full-time work) and a (the actual work must primarily involve executive, administrative, or professional responsibilities requiring independent judgment). Most personal assistant work, scheduling, errands, correspondence, coordination, doesn't independently satisfy the duties test, even when the pay meets the salary threshold.

Both tests have to be satisfied at the same time, meeting one doesn't excuse the other. An assistant paid well above the salary threshold can still be non-exempt if the day-to-day job doesn't involve the kind of independent judgment the duties test requires. Conversely, a role with genuinely exempt-level responsibilities but a salary below the threshold also fails the test and remains non-exempt.

The duties test looks past the job description to what actually happens most weeks. An assistant who occasionally makes a judgment call about scheduling conflicts, but who spends the overwhelming majority of the workweek on routine, non-discretionary tasks, generally still fails the duties test, since the exemption depends on the primary duty, not on isolated moments of independent decision-making.

3

Common Red Flags in Personal Assistant Pay

A few patterns are worth noticing: a flat day rate that doesn't change no matter how long the day runs, a salary that gets docked for partial days off (which can actually undermine an exemption claim rather than support it), and a job title like "Personal Assistant" or "Executive Assistant" used as if the title alone settles the question. None of these determine exempt status on their own, the actual duties do.

Another common red flag is a pay structure that changes shape depending on what work gets done that week, an hourly rate for "office days" and a flat rate for "travel days," for instance. Mixing pay structures like this doesn't by itself violate the law, but it often signals that the employer hasn't thought through how overtime should actually be calculated across those different arrangements, which is exactly the kind of gap that leads to underpayment.

Watch also for offer letters or contracts that describe the role as "exempt" without any real explanation of why. A written classification isn't a legal conclusion, it's just the employer's position, and that position can be wrong. If your actual day-to-day work looks like scheduling, correspondence, and errands rather than genuine executive or administrative decision-making, the label in your contract doesn't settle the question.

4

What Misclassified Pay Could Mean for Back Wages

If a personal assistant has been paid a flat salary or day rate while performing non-exempt duties, unpaid overtime can add up quickly over months or years of employment, and may include interest and penalties on top of the wages themselves. Use our pay raise calculator or take-home pay estimator to get a sense of what your hours should be worth, then start a free case review if the gap looks significant.

Because misclassification usually isn't a single mistake but a pattern repeated pay period after pay period, back wages tend to compound rather than stay small. A few unpaid overtime hours a week might not seem like much in isolation, but multiplied across a year or more of employment, and combined with potential penalties for late or inaccurate wage statements, the total can be far larger than most assistants initially expect.

Even if you're unsure whether your specific duties clear the bar for an exemption, that uncertainty alone is a good reason to have your pay structure reviewed rather than assume your employer got the classification right. The duties test is fact-specific, and a short conversation about what your actual workweek looks like is often enough to tell whether the salary or day rate you're receiving lines up with what California law requires.
