Can Restaurant Cooks Be Required to Prep Before Clocking In?

If a manager requires it, it's paid time, no matter what the kitchen calls it.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR

Mise en place and other prep work a kitchen requires before a cook's official shift starts is compensable work time under California law. "That's just what cooks do" isn't a legal exception, and unpaid prep time can also trigger unpaid overtime once the real hours are counted.

Every line cook knows the drill: get in early, get stations set, get ready before service starts. That's kitchen culture in restaurants across San Diego, Sacramento, and Beverly Hills, and a lot of kitchens still treat that early prep time as something cooks are simply expected to donate.

Share this article:
01

Mise en Place Is Still Work

Chopping vegetables, portioning proteins, prepping sauces, and setting up a station are all work, regardless of the French culinary term attached to the routine. If a kitchen manager expects a cook's station to be ready by a certain time, and that readiness requires work beforehand, that work has to be paid.

02

The Same Control Test That Applies Everywhere Else

California uses the same control-based standard here as it does for retail bag checks or hotel cart prep: if the employer requires the activity and controls how or when it happens, it's compensable time. A cook told to arrive 30 minutes before the posted shift to have a station prepped is, as a matter of law, working during that 30 minutes, whether or not the time clock reflects it.

03

How This Turns Into an Overtime Problem

Unpaid prep time doesn't just shortchange straight-time pay, it also hides overtime. A cook who prepped for 45 minutes off the clock and then worked a full eight-hour shift on the books actually worked 8 hours and 45 minutes that day. That extra 45 minutes should be paid at the overtime rate if it's the tail end of the day pushing total hours past 8, not folded silently into an unpaid warm-up routine.

04

Who This Affects Most

Line cooks and prep cooks at high-volume, reservation-driven restaurants in Beverly Hills and San Diego, where a slow start to service isn't tolerated, tend to face the most pressure to arrive early unpaid. Kitchen staff at hotel restaurants and banquet kitchens in San Francisco and Sacramento often deal with early prep tied to specific event start times that leave little room to negotiate. And cooks at smaller, owner-operated restaurants across Fountain Valley and San Bernardino frequently work under an informal "just get here early" culture with no time tracking for that period at all.

05

Common Violations

Watch for a posted shift start time that doesn't match when you're actually expected to be prepped and ready, no clock-in option before the official shift start even when prep is required, and total weekly hours that would trigger overtime once real prep time is counted honestly.

06

What to Do Next

Track the actual time you arrive and start working versus your official clock-in time over a couple of weeks. This connects to the same off-the-clock issue we cover in work before you clock in and our hotel housekeeper cart prep guide.
