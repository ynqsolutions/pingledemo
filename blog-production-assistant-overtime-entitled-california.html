Are Production Assistants Entitled to Overtime in California?

A flat day rate doesn't replace overtime math, it's supposed to be built on top of it.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

Almost all production assistants are non-exempt employees entitled to overtime under California law: 1.5x pay after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week, and double time after 12 hours in a day. Being paid a flat day rate or being told the job is "exempt" doesn't change that math, and it applies the same on a studio feature as it does on a two-day independent shoot.

Production assistant work is often someone's first job in the industry, and that entry-level status gets mistaken for a reason overtime rules don't apply. They do. A PA who's paid hourly, by the day, or even in cash is still generally protected by the same California overtime law that covers a warehouse worker or a retail employee, and long, unpredictable call times make it more important to understand that math, not less.

1

The Default Answer: Yes

Most production assistants are s under California law, entitled to for hours worked beyond 8 in a day or 40 in a week, and double time beyond 12 hours. There is no blanket "entertainment industry exemption" that removes PAs from these rules. Exemption depends on actual job duties, not on how junior the role is or what the production calls itself.

This applies equally whether a PA is working on a studio feature, a reality series, or a small independent film with a skeleton crew. The size and prestige of the production has no bearing on whether overtime rules apply.

Example

A PA on a two-day independent short film is told the whole cast and crew are working for "exposure and a flat fee." That arrangement doesn't erase the overtime owed once the day runs past 8 hours.

2

Why PAs Are Almost Never Exempt

California's overtime exemptions generally require substantial independent discretion, supervisory authority, or specialized judgment, plus a minimum salary threshold. A PA running errands or managing paperwork under a coordinator's direction typically doesn't meet that bar, regardless of the title on the call sheet. The minimum salary threshold alone disqualifies a lot of PA positions from any exemption analysis in the first place, since many are paid well below the level California requires.

Job titles in production are notoriously inconsistent, and a title alone never determines exemption status. A "key PA" title doesn't confer the kind of independent managerial authority the law requires, no matter how it sounds on a deal memo. If a PA position doesn't clear the salary floor, the question of whether the duties might otherwise qualify for an exemption doesn't even need to be reached.

3

Day Rates and the Overtime Math Underneath Them

A flat day rate is legal only if it's structured to still account for overtime once the day runs long. An employer generally has to calculate an hourly rate from the day rate, then pay 1.5x and 2x separately for hours beyond 8 and 12. A day rate that pays exactly the same amount whether the day runs 10 hours or 15 is a common and often illegal shortcut, not a legitimate alternative to hourly pay.

Example

A $200 day rate for an 8-hour day works out to a $25 hourly rate. If the day runs 12 hours, the worker should generally get the original $200 plus 1.5x $25 for hours 9 through 12, not just $200 flat.

This same principle applies whether the arrangement is called a "flat rate," a "project rate," or simply cash handed out at wrap. None of those labels change the underlying requirement that pay reflect actual hours at the correct overtime multipliers.

4

Double Time and the Seventh Consecutive Day

Double time applies to hours beyond 12 in a single day, and separately to hours worked beyond 8 on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek. Production schedules that run six or seven days straight before a wrap date can trigger this second double-time rule even on a day that isn't especially long by itself, which is easy to miss if payroll only looks at total hours for the week.

This rule is calculated against the employer's defined workweek, not a rolling seven-day window from whenever a PA happened to start, so it helps to know your production's official workweek to tell when the threshold actually applies. A PA who worked six 8-hour days and is then called in for even a short seventh day should generally see double time kick in after the first 8 hours of that day.

5

Common Excuses Productions Use

A few justifications come up repeatedly and don't hold up: "everyone in this industry works this way," "you're getting a flat rate so overtime doesn't apply," and "this is how you get the next job." None of these change a non-exempt employee's legal entitlement to overtime, no matter how competitive or relationship-driven the hiring culture is, or how informal the payroll practices on a given production happen to be.

Sometimes this shows up as friendly advice rather than formal policy, a coordinator telling a new PA not to bother tracking hours because "that's not really how it works here." That kind of informal discouragement doesn't change what the law requires a production to pay, even when it's framed as helpful guidance rather than a policy.

6

How to Check What You're Actually Owed

Keep your own record of call time and wrap time for every shoot day, since a day rate paycheck rarely shows the underlying hours. Run those hours through our Overtime Pay Calculator to see what a properly calculated day should have paid, then compare that to what you actually received. A consistent gap across multiple shoot days is a pattern worth having reviewed.

Keep this record somewhere durable, a spreadsheet or a running note updated the same day rather than reconstructed weeks later from memory once a dispute is already underway. Even a pay stub that shows only a lump day rate with no hourly breakdown is itself informative, since it suggests the underlying overtime math likely wasn't performed in the first place.
