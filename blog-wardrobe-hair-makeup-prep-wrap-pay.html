Do Wardrobe, Hair, and Makeup Workers Get Paid for Prep and Wrap Time?

If the production requires it before the official day starts, the pay is supposed to start there too.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

Setting up a station, prepping supplies, and breaking down a kit before or after the official call time is generally compensable work under California law if the production requires it. Pay is supposed to reflect actual hours worked, not just the window between a printed call time and wrap time. Unpaid prep and wrap time can also hide unpaid overtime once the real hours are counted correctly.

Hair, makeup, and wardrobe departments routinely arrive before the rest of the crew and leave after everyone else has gone home, setting up stations, organizing supplies, and breaking everything down at the end of the day. That extra time is real work, required by the production to make the shoot day function, and it's supposed to be paid the same as any other work time. Because this prep and wrap work often happens off to the side, in a trailer or a designated tent rather than on the main set floor, it's also one of the easiest categories of work time for a production to lose track of when payroll is calculated.

1

Prep and Wrap Are Work, Not Favors

California uses a broad test for what counts as compensable work: whether the employer knew or should have known the work was happening, known as being . Setting up a wardrobe rack, sanitizing brushes, prepping a makeup station, or breaking down and inventorying a kit after wrap is work under that standard, whether or not it appears on an official schedule, because the production requires it to function.

This standard exists specifically to prevent employers from benefiting from work they know is happening simply because it wasn't formally logged or scheduled. A production doesn't need to explicitly say "please come in early to prep" for that early prep time to count as compensable, if the production knows, or reasonably should know, that hair and makeup routinely arrives ahead of the official call time to be ready, that knowledge is enough to trigger the pay obligation.

This is a meaningfully different legal standard than requiring proof that a supervisor explicitly instructed the early arrival. If a coordinator sees the same department consistently show up 90 minutes early every single day of a shoot and never raises an objection, that pattern alone is generally enough to establish the production's knowledge of, and acquiescence to, that unpaid early work.

2

Call Time vs. When Pay Is Actually Supposed to Start

A printed "call time" on a call sheet is often treated as the moment pay starts, but if a department is required to arrive earlier to be ready by that call time, the earlier arrival is itself compensable. The legal question is when the work actually began, not what time is printed on the schedule. A hair and makeup team required to be "camera ready" at 6 a.m. that means arriving at 4:30 to prep should generally be paid from 4:30, not 6.

This gap between the printed call time and the actual start of work is often baked into a production's scheduling without anyone consciously deciding to shortchange pay, it simply becomes an assumed part of how the department operates. That doesn't make it lawful. If the production is relying on that early arrival to make its schedule work, the department's actual working hours start at the real arrival time, and pay should reflect that.

The same logic runs in reverse at the end of the day. If the schedule shows a wrap time but the department is still expected to be on set finishing touch-ups, restocking, or breaking down well after that printed time, pay is supposed to extend to the actual finish, not stop at whatever time appears on the paperwork.

3

Kit Fees and Rate Confusion

Many wardrobe, hair, and makeup professionals receive a separate kit fee or rental fee for the equipment and supplies they bring and maintain. That fee is meant to compensate for the cost and use of the kit itself, not to cover the labor time spent prepping, maintaining, or breaking it down. Folding kit fee payments into an argument that prep and wrap time is "already covered" is a common point of confusion that doesn't hold up when the two are legally distinct.

Think of the kit fee as compensation for the equipment, brushes, product, rolling cases, the wear and depreciation on items the worker personally owns and brings to set, separate entirely from the labor of using and maintaining that equipment. A production paying a generous kit fee while leaving prep and wrap labor unpaid is effectively compensating for the tools but not the person using them, which doesn't satisfy the separate wage obligation for the time actually worked.

Some productions also try to bundle the kit fee directly into an inflated day rate rather than listing it separately, which can make it harder after the fact to identify how much of the total payment was actually meant for labor versus equipment. Asking for kit fees and labor rates to be itemized separately in writing before a job starts can help avoid this confusion later.

4

Union vs. Non-Union Differences

Union agreements covering hair, makeup, and costume departments often specify prep and wrap time explicitly, sometimes building in a fixed period before and after the shoot day. On non-union productions, those same protections still exist under general California wage and hour law, they're just not spelled out in a specific contract, which is exactly why they get overlooked more often on smaller or non-union projects.

The specificity of a union agreement, naming an exact number of prep and wrap minutes and building them directly into the daily pay calculation, is part of what makes those provisions harder to quietly ignore. On a non-union set, the same underlying legal protection exists, but without that explicit contractual language spelling it out, it's much easier for prep and wrap time to simply go untracked and unpaid by default.

Even on a union production, it's worth checking that the actual pay stub reflects the specific prep and wrap minutes the agreement calls for, since a contractual entitlement on paper doesn't automatically guarantee that payroll applied it correctly in practice, particularly on a production juggling many different department agreements at once.

5

Common Violations in Hair, Makeup, and Wardrobe Departments

Frequent patterns include being told to arrive early "informally" without that time being logged, being required to stay late for strike and inventory without pay reflecting it, and a day rate that doesn't account for the actual span between true start and true finish. Because these departments often work in a separate trailer or tent away from the main set, this time can go unnoticed by production management even when it's happening consistently.

Wrap time tends to be even more consistently underpaid than prep time, since the rest of the crew is often released and heading home while wardrobe, hair, and makeup are still breaking down stations, cleaning brushes, and doing inventory for the next day. That gap between the crew's official wrap time and the department's actual finish time is real, unpaid work in most cases, even when nobody on set treats it that way.

Multi-cast productions add another layer to this problem, where a department handling several actors back to back may finish their last touch-up well after the general crew wrap time was logged, simply because the schedule required staggered call and release times that were never reflected in how the department itself was paid.

6

Documenting Your Actual Hours

Track your true start time (when you actually begin prepping, not the printed call time) and your true finish time (when kit breakdown and inventory are actually done), every day, not just when something feels unusually long. Run a sample week through our Overtime Pay Calculator to see whether that additional prep and wrap time changes your overtime calculation, and if there's a consistent gap between hours worked and hours paid, that's worth raising or having reviewed.

A simple daily note of your actual arrival and departure times, kept consistently rather than reconstructed later, becomes powerful evidence precisely because it shows a pattern rather than a single disputed day. If that pattern reveals unpaid time adding up to real overtime across a multi-week shoot, that's a significant amount of money worth pursuing rather than writing off as just how the job works.

Photos of your setup at the actual start of your day and the actual state of the kit at true wrap can also help corroborate a written log, especially on productions where paper timecards are informal or inconsistently collected.
