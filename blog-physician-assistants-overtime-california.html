Do Physician Assistants Get Overtime in California?

Often, yes. Being a licensed provider doesn't automatically mean you're exempt from overtime pay.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR

Physician assistants are frequently, and often incorrectly, classified as exempt from overtime. Whether you're actually exempt depends on your salary and what you do day to day, not your license or job title. Many PAs in California are owed significant back overtime.

Physician assistants occupy an odd space in a lot of practices and hospital systems: treated like a physician for scheduling and workload purposes, but without the compensation structure, or the legal exemption, that actually applies to physicians. We regularly hear from PAs across Fountain Valley, San Diego, and Sacramento who assumed their license meant no overtime, full stop. That assumption is often wrong.

Share this article:
01

Why "Licensed Provider" Doesn't Mean Exempt

California recognizes a specific "learned professional" exemption that can apply to certain licensed roles, but qualifying for it takes more than holding a professional license. It requires work that's predominantly intellectual, requires advanced knowledge in a field of science, and involves consistent exercise of discretion and independent judgment, combined with a salary that meets California's minimum exempt threshold (which is tied to the state minimum wage and is higher than many employers assume).

A PA who spends most of a shift following physician-directed protocols, seeing a high volume of standardized visits, or working under close supervision may not meet that discretion requirement, regardless of the "Physician Assistant" title on their badge. This is a classic pattern.

02

The Salary Test PAs Often Fail

Even PAs whose duties genuinely involve independent judgment still have to clear California's minimum salary threshold for exemption, which is recalculated periodically and is meaningfully higher than the federal minimum. Some smaller practices and urgent care groups, especially newer clinics expanding across Orange County and the Inland Empire, pay PAs a salary that sits below this threshold while still treating them as exempt. If your salary doesn't clear that bar, you're entitled to overtime no matter how independent your work is.

03

Call Coverage and Extended Shifts

PAs frequently take on call coverage, urgent same-day add-ons, or extended shifts to cover physician absences, often without any adjustment to pay. If you're a PA, all of that additional time is compensable, including on-call time where you're required to remain reachable and able to respond within a short window.

04

Who This Affects Most

Emergency medicine and urgent care PAs tend to see the heaviest volume-driven overtime, since patient load rather than the clock determines when a shift actually ends. PAs working in surgical specialties often face early call times and post-op rounding that stretch well beyond a scheduled shift. And PAs employed by fast-growing multi-site urgent care chains across San Bernardino, Beverly Hills, and San Francisco are especially likely to be affected, since standardized corporate pay policies don't always reflect each PA's actual duties at each location.

05

Common Violations

Watch for a flat annual salary that never changes regardless of hours worked, no overtime line ever appearing on a pay stub, unpaid on-call time, and being asked to "clock out" for administrative tasks like chart review or prior authorizations that are still required work.

06

What to Do Next

Pull your last several pay stubs and your actual job description, then compare what you do day to day against what your compensation structure assumes. This overlaps closely with our broader unpaid wages and overtime guidance, and with the misclassification issues we see across nurse practitioner pay as well. A free case review can look at your specific role and compensation, wherever in California you're practicing.
