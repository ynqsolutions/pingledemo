My Construction Boss Pays Me Cash. What Are My Rights in California?

Cash pay might feel informal, but your legal rights don't disappear just because there's no pay stub.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

Being paid in cash doesn't remove your rights to minimum wage, overtime, and other wage protections under California law. It often signals that your employer isn't properly tracking hours or issuing legally required wage statements, which can actually make it easier, not harder, to show violations once you reconstruct your own records.

A lot of smaller construction crews still pay cash at the end of the week, and workers often assume that means the usual wage rules don't apply, or that there's no way to prove what happened without official records. Neither is true. Here's what California law still requires, cash or no cash, and how to protect yourself if that's how you're paid, whether you're new to this arrangement or have been paid this way for years.

01

Cash Pay Doesn't Change Your Legal Status

Whether you're paid by direct deposit, check, or cash in an envelope at the end of the week, you're still entitled to the same minimum wage, overtime, meal and rest break, and wage statement protections as any other California employee, as long as you're actually functioning as an employee rather than a genuine independent contractor.

A lot of workers assume that because there's no formal paperwork, there's also no legal protection, but that assumption has it backwards. The law looks at the actual working relationship, not the payment method, to determine what you're owed.

02

Why Employers Pay Cash

Employers pay cash for a lot of reasons, some more concerning than others. Sometimes it's simply how a small crew has always operated. Other times, cash pay is used specifically to avoid payroll taxes, workers' compensation coverage, and the paper trail that would otherwise document overtime and wage violations.

Cash pay also sometimes shows up alongside a preference for hiring workers who feel they have less leverage to complain, whether due to immigration status, unfamiliarity with California labor law, or simply needing the work badly enough not to ask questions. None of those circumstances change what the law actually requires.

It's worth recognizing that being paid cash doesn't automatically mean an employer is acting in bad faith, some genuinely just haven't set up formal payroll. But good intentions don't substitute for the legal obligations that still apply either way.

Example

You're handed $600 cash at the end of a 50-hour week with no explanation of how that number breaks down into hourly pay, overtime, or anything else, just a flat amount.

03

The Wage Statement Requirement Still Applies

California law requires employers to provide an itemized wage statement every pay period, showing hours worked, pay rate, gross wages, deductions, and other required details, regardless of whether you're paid by cash, check, or deposit. Cash pay with no accompanying wage statement is, on its own, a separate wage violation.

Even a simple handwritten receipt showing hours and pay would be better than nothing, but most cash-pay arrangements provide no documentation at all. That gap doesn't excuse the employer, it simply means you may need to build your own record instead of relying on theirs.

04

Misclassification Often Comes Along With Cash Pay

Cash pay often travels alongside , being labeled an when you're actually functioning as an employee. California uses the ABC test to determine correct classification, and simply calling someone a contractor and paying them cash doesn't make that label accurate if your employer controls your schedule, tools, and how the work gets done.

The ABC test generally asks whether you're free from the employer's control, whether your work is outside the employer's usual business, and whether you customarily run an independent business doing that same kind of work. A construction laborer directed daily by a foreman rarely satisfies all three parts of that test, regardless of what they're called.

Example

Your 'boss' tells you what time to show up, provides the tools and materials, assigns you to specific tasks throughout the day, and pays you cash weekly, but calls you an independent contractor with no written agreement of any kind.

05

How to Reconstruct Your Records

Without pay stubs, reconstructing your own record becomes important. Bank deposit patterns, text messages about schedules or pay, photos from job sites with timestamps, and your own daily log of hours worked and cash received can all help establish a pattern even without formal employer records.

Witnesses matter here too. Coworkers who can confirm your typical hours, your rate of pay, and who directed your work each day can meaningfully strengthen a case built primarily on your own records, especially if several people independently describe a consistent picture.

06

Common Violations We See

Common violations we see with cash-paid construction workers include pay that falls under minimum wage once real hours are calculated, no overtime premium on longer days, no wage statements at all, and workers who are functionally employees but labeled as independent contractors specifically to avoid these obligations.

We also frequently see cash-paid workers denied workers' compensation coverage after a job site injury, on the theory that they were never really employees to begin with. That position often doesn't hold up once the actual working relationship is examined closely.

07

Protecting Yourself Going Forward

If you're currently in a cash-pay arrangement and want to keep the job while also protecting yourself, it's reasonable to start asking for something in writing, even a simple text message each week confirming hours and pay, without necessarily demanding a full overhaul of how the crew operates.

It's also worth understanding that raising these questions is itself a protected activity in many circumstances, meaning an employer who suddenly cuts your hours or lets you go shortly after you start asking about proper pay records may be creating a separate retaliation problem on top of the original wage issue.

08

Who This Affects Most

This affects day laborers and smaller residential crews throughout California most often, especially newer workers or those without formal immigration status who may feel less able to push back on how they're paid. It connects to the broader wage issues we cover in our guides on construction overtime and flat day-rate pay, both of which apply just as much to cash-paid workers as anyone else.

Workers hired informally through word of mouth, without any application, interview, or written agreement, are especially common in this category, and the informality of how the job started doesn't change the legal obligations that attach to it once work actually begins.

09

What to Do Next

Start keeping your own simple log now, dates, hours, and cash amounts received, even if it feels informal. Your immigration status does not affect your right to be paid correctly under California law, regardless of what an employer may imply to discourage you from asking questions.

A free case review can help you understand your options and next steps, wherever in California you're working, and can also help you understand what workers' compensation coverage you may be entitled to if you've been injured on a cash-paid job.
