1099 or Employee? What California Entertainment Workers Should Know

A 1099 tells you how you were paid. It doesn't tell you how you're supposed to be classified.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 8 min read

TL;DR

Receiving a 1099 doesn't automatically make someone an independent contractor in California, classification is determined by California's ABC test, not by which tax form a production issues. A lot of entertainment work, crew positions, background work, and many freelance production roles, fails that test and should legally be treated as employment. Misclassification can cost a worker overtime, meal and rest break premiums, reimbursed expenses, and unemployment insurance eligibility.

Independent contractor arrangements are common in entertainment, and for genuinely independent work, that can be perfectly appropriate. The problem is how often the label gets applied to work that doesn't actually meet California's legal test for it. A production issuing a 1099 instead of a W-2 is a business decision, not a legal determination, and workers who assume the tax form settles the question are often leaving real protections on the table. Because so many entertainment careers are built around short-term, project-based engagements, this question comes up constantly, and the answer is rarely as simple as whichever tax form happened to show up at the end of the year.

1

Why This Question Matters So Much in Entertainment

Freelance culture runs deep in film, television, and content production, short-term projects, per-job hiring, and a workforce that moves between productions constantly. That environment makes it easy for a to blend into normal industry practice. But classification isn't about industry custom, it's a legal question with real financial consequences, and entertainment work doesn't get a different set of rules just because contractor arrangements are common there.

This matters even more for workers who string together multiple short gigs across a year, since a pattern of misclassification compounds quickly. Losing overtime, break premiums, and unemployment insurance eligibility on one two-week job might feel minor in isolation, but the same pattern repeated across five or six productions in a year adds up to a meaningful amount of money and lost protection, all built on the same underlying legal error.

It's also worth understanding that a production doesn't get to simply choose the classification it prefers based on which is cheaper or more convenient administratively. California law treats classification as a factual question determined by the actual nature of the working relationship, not a label either side is free to pick, which is exactly why a worker's own understanding of the real working arrangement matters so much in sorting this out.

2

California's ABC Test

California uses the to determine whether a worker is properly classified as an . A hiring production has to show all three prongs: the worker controls how the work gets done, the work is outside the production's usual business, and the worker independently operates their own established trade doing similar work for other clients. A crew member directed by a department head, doing core production work, who works exclusively for one production at a time, is a difficult fit for that test.

The "B" prong, whether the work is outside the hiring entity's usual business, tends to be the hardest one for productions to satisfy honestly. A production company's usual business is making the film or show, so a grip, an editor, a camera operator, or a script supervisor is performing exactly the kind of work the business exists to do, not some outside specialty service unrelated to the production itself. That alone is often enough to sink a contractor classification regardless of how the other two prongs shake out.

The "C" prong is also frequently misunderstood. It requires the worker to have an actual, existing independent business, one that markets its services to multiple clients, not simply the fact that a worker is technically available to take other jobs someday. A crew member who has only ever worked for one production company, with no other active clients or an established, independently marketed trade, generally doesn't satisfy this prong even if nothing legally prevented them from seeking other work.

3

Common Red Flags on Productions

Watch for a worker who's told when and where to show up, follows a call sheet set by someone else, uses equipment and space the production provides, works exclusively on that production during the engagement, and performs work that's core to actually making the film or show, not a specialized outside service. Any one of these alone isn't decisive, but together they describe control and integration that looks a lot more like employment than independent contracting.

Another common red flag is a worker who was previously classified as a W-2 employee for the same or similar role, sometimes even at the same production company, and was later switched to 1099 status without any real change in how the job is actually performed. When the day-to-day work is identical but the classification changes, that's a signal the shift was about reducing payroll costs and paperwork, not about a genuine change in the nature of the working relationship.

A worker asked to sign a contractor agreement as a precondition of being hired at all, with no room to negotiate its terms, is also worth noticing. Genuine independent contractors typically have some ability to negotiate their own rates and terms across different clients, while a "take it or leave it" contractor agreement offered to every worker in a given role looks a lot more like a standardized employment relationship dressed up in contractor language.

4

What Misclassification Actually Costs You

A worker misclassified as a contractor generally loses access to overtime pay, meal and rest break premiums, reimbursement for job-related expenses, workers' compensation coverage if they're injured, unemployment insurance if the job ends, and employer-side payroll tax contributions. It also shifts the full self-employment tax burden onto the worker. These aren't minor administrative differences, they represent real money and real protections that a correctly classified employee would otherwise have.

The workers' compensation piece is worth sitting with in particular, since production work carries real physical risk, rigging, lighting equipment, stunt-adjacent work, and long hours around heavy gear. A misclassified contractor injured on set can find themselves without the coverage that would normally apply to an employee doing that exact same job, at exactly the moment that coverage would matter most.

The unemployment insurance gap tends to matter most in an industry defined by gaps between jobs. A properly classified employee who wraps one production and doesn't immediately land the next one has unemployment benefits as a bridge, while a misclassified worker in the exact same situation has no such safety net, simply because their last job was labeled a contractor engagement rather than employment.

The reimbursement piece is also easy to overlook but adds up quickly in production work, where contractors are often expected to cover their own gas, parking, equipment, and supplies without any reimbursement at all. A properly classified employee is generally entitled to reimbursement for necessary business expenses incurred while doing the job, an obligation that disappears entirely once a worker is treated as a contractor.

5

Union Status and Loan-Out Complications

Union membership and loan-out corporations add a layer of complexity but don't automatically resolve the classification question. A loan-out arrangement, where a worker is technically employed by their own corporation that then contracts with the production, is a legitimate structure in some contexts, but it can also be used to obscure what is functionally an employment relationship with the production itself. Whether a specific arrangement is genuine or a workaround depends on the actual facts, not just the paperwork.

Loan-out structures are common and legitimate for higher-earning above-the-line talent, where the arrangement genuinely reflects an independent business relationship negotiated by representatives on both sides. The same structure looks very different when it's used for a below-the-line crew member who had no real negotiating leverage and was simply told to set up an LLC as a condition of getting hired, with day-to-day work that looks identical to every W-2 employee around them.

6

What to Do If You Think You're Misclassified

Document the actual working relationship: who directed your schedule, what equipment you used, whether you worked for other productions at the same time, and how integrated your role was into the production itself. If that picture doesn't match a genuinely independent business relationship, it's worth having your specific situation reviewed, since correcting a misclassification can recover more than just back pay going forward.

It also helps to gather any 1099s or contracts from the engagement, along with call sheets, schedules, and any written instructions from production management showing the level of control actually exercised over your work. The stronger the paper trail showing genuine employer control, the clearer the case for correcting the classification and recovering what should have been paid all along.

Because these claims often involve multiple overlapping issues, unpaid overtime, missed breaks, and unreimbursed expenses on top of the classification question itself, it's worth having the full picture reviewed together rather than addressing each piece separately. A single review can usually identify every category of pay that should be corrected once the underlying misclassification is established.
