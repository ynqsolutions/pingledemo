Is It Illegal for Your Employer to Misgender You or Use the Wrong Name at Work in California?

One honest slip is usually not unlawful, but repeated, deliberate misuse of your name or pronouns can be harassment.

TL;DR

Sometimes. California protects gender identity and gender expression, and repeatedly and intentionally using the wrong name or pronouns after you have asked can be harassment. An honest one-time mistake usually is not. Tell your employer in writing, keep records, and know that speaking up is protected.

Being called the wrong name or pronoun at work can feel small to others and heavy to you. The law does not treat every slip as a violation, but it does take a pattern seriously. Below, we explain when misgendering or deadnaming becomes harassment, what employers can and cannot require, and how to protect yourself.

1

When It Becomes Harassment

California protects gender identity and gender expression. is unwelcome conduct because of a protected trait. A coworker who mixes up your pronouns once and apologizes is generally not breaking the law. The picture changes when or is repeated, intentional, or continues after you clearly asked the person to stop. Conduct by a supervisor is taken more seriously, and one very serious incident can be enough.

Example

A nurse tells her manager she goes by Maya and uses the pronouns she and her. For months the manager keeps using her former name in meetings, even after she asks again in writing. A pattern like this is more likely to count as harassment than a single slip.

2

Your Name and Pronouns Day to Day

Employers generally should use the name and pronouns you ask for in daily work, including in email, schedules, name tags and meetings. They may still need your legal name for payroll, tax and benefits records, and that alone is not discrimination. The concern is refusing to use your chosen name where it would be easy to do so. Ask HR whether your chosen name can appear in your email address, the directory and your badge. If a payroll system or software still shows your former name, ask for a fix and a date it will be done, and keep the reply.

3

Restrooms, Dress Codes and Privacy

California law generally lets employees use restrooms and locker rooms that match their gender identity, and employers should not require you to dress in a way that conflicts with your identity or expression. Your gender identity, sexual orientation or transition status is also your information to share. Telling coworkers without your permission can be part of a harassment claim. See our guide on workplace behavior that crosses the line.

Example

A cashier is told to use the restroom “for his own sex” and is reprimanded after using the one that matches his identity. A restriction like that may be unlawful, and he should write down who gave the instruction and when.

4

Speaking Up and What Employers Must Do

Once you tell a manager or HR, the employer has a duty to take reasonable steps to stop harassment. Punishing you for speaking up, with worse shifts, a poor review or a firing, is , which can be unlawful even if the original complaint is not proven. Many employers must also train supervisors on harassment prevention, including harassment based on gender identity. Learn more about workplace harassment in California.

5

What to Do

Keep a simple log with the date, what was said, who heard it and how you responded. Ask once, clearly and politely, and put the request in writing. Save emails, chats and schedules that show the wrong name. If it continues, report it to HR in writing and keep a copy. In California you generally have three years to file with the Civil Rights Department.

Avoid arguing in public or replying in anger, since a calm record is stronger. If you are unsure whether a situation counts, an attorney can review it with you in confidence.
