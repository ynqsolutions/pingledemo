Do Federal Changes to LGBTQ Protections Affect Your Job in California? What Employees Should Know

Federal rules and enforcement priorities can change. California law protects you separately.

TL;DR

Federal changes generally do not remove your protections in California. State law separately bars discrimination based on sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression, and an executive order cannot by itself overturn a Supreme Court decision. What can change is how federal agencies handle complaints, so keep records and know your state options.

News about federal policy on LGBTQ issues leaves many workers wondering whether their job protections have changed. For most California employees, state law still applies. The details depend on where you work and who you work for, so below we explain what stays the same, what may change, and what you can do.

1

What Stays the Same

California's FEHA protects sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression, and it applies no matter what happens in Washington. Federal law still includes Title VII, and the Supreme Court's 2020 Bostock decision read it to cover gay and transgender workers. An directs federal agencies, but it cannot by itself overturn a statute or a Supreme Court ruling. That means the core state protections still apply to most California employers, whatever the federal government decides about enforcing its own rules. See our overview of workplace discrimination in California.

2

What May Change

Federal agencies decide which complaints to pursue and how they explain the law in guidance and training. Those priorities can shift between administrations, which may affect how the handles a charge, how quickly, or what help it offers. Rules for federal contractors and federal employees can also differ from those for private employers. These changes affect process more than the underlying right.

If a federal agency slows down or declines a case, that does not end your options. You can still file with the state agency, go to court after receiving a right-to-sue notice, and use your employer's own complaint process. Employers also remain bound by their own written anti-discrimination policies.

3

Where California Fits In

The enforces FEHA, takes complaints directly, and can issue a right-to-sue notice so you can go to court. Because that process does not depend on federal priorities, many California employees file with the state, and a complaint can often be shared with the EEOC as well. You do not need to wait for a federal agency to act before using the state process. Learn how the federal route works in our guide on filing an EEOC complaint.

Example

A call-center employee in San Diego hears that federal enforcement is changing and wonders whether to report a manager's slurs. Her California protections have not changed, and she can still report to HR and file with the Civil Rights Department.

4

Deadlines and Staying Protected

In California you generally have three years to file most FEHA claims with the Civil Rights Department, and about 300 days for the EEOC. Federal employees follow a separate process with much shorter deadlines, often 45 days to contact an EEO counselor. Acting sooner keeps evidence fresh. Retaliation for reporting discrimination is also unlawful.

Example

A retail worker waits eight months to report a manager's repeated slurs because he heard protections were gone. He is still within the state deadline, but each month makes witnesses harder to find, so he starts gathering messages and names now.

5

What to Do

Keep records of what happened, report problems in writing, and do not assume you have lost protection because of a headline. Because this area changes, the rules that apply to you may depend on your employer and the date, so get advice on your specific facts. Check your handbook and anti-discrimination policy, which often promise more than the minimum, and note who handles complaints. If you work for the federal government or a federal contractor, mention that when you reach out, since different rules and deadlines may apply.
