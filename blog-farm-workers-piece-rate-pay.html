Can Farm Workers Be Paid by the Piece Instead of by the Hour in California?

Getting paid per bin or per row is common in the fields, but California law puts real limits on how piece-rate pay can work.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

Yes, piece-rate pay is legal for California farm workers, but it must still average out to at least minimum wage for every hour worked, and rest breaks and other nonproductive time must be paid separately, on top of piece-rate earnings, not folded into them.

Getting paid per bin, per row, or per tray is a long-standing tradition in California agriculture, and it's still legal today. But piece-rate pay comes with strict guardrails under California law, and it's one of the most frequently miscalculated pay structures we see, especially when it comes to breaks. Understanding exactly how the rules are supposed to work makes it much easier to spot when a paycheck doesn't add up.

Share this article:
01

What Piece-Rate Pay Actually Means

means you're paid a set amount per unit of work, per bin of strawberries, per row planted, per tray packed, rather than a straight hourly wage. It's common across California agriculture because it can reward faster, more productive workers, but it's also one of the most frequently miscalculated pay structures we see.

Growers often like piece-rate pay because it ties labor cost directly to output, and many experienced workers genuinely earn more this way than they would on a straight hourly wage. The system itself isn't the problem, it's the specific rules layered on top of it that frequently get skipped.

02

The Minimum Wage Floor Still Applies

No matter how piece-rate pay is structured, your total earnings for the workweek must average out to at least minimum wage for every hour you worked. If your piece-rate earnings fall short of that floor in a given pay period, your employer is required to make up the difference.

This calculation has to be done honestly, using your actual hours worked, not an estimate or a standard assumption about how long a task 'should' take. A grower can't simply assume every worker picks at the same pace and skip verifying actual output against actual hours.

Example

You pick enough to earn $180 in piece-rate pay across a 10-hour day. Minimum wage for 10 hours would be roughly $160 to $170 depending on the local rate, so you'd clear the floor that day. A slower day where piece-rate earnings fall under that threshold requires your employer to top up your pay.

03

Rest Breaks Must Be Paid Separately

One of the biggest piece-rate mistakes involves rest breaks. California law requires piece-rate workers to be paid separately, at a specified minimum rate, for their rest breaks, on top of what they earned through piece-rate work. Employers can't simply assume your piece-rate earnings already cover break time.

This rule exists specifically because piece-rate work creates an incentive to skip breaks entirely, since time spent resting is time not spent earning. California addressed that incentive directly by requiring separate, guaranteed pay for rest time regardless of how much piece-rate work got done that day.

Example

You take your legally required 10-minute rest break twice during an 8-hour shift, but your pay stub shows no separate line item for that paid rest time, only your total piece-rate earnings for the day.

04

Other Nonproductive Time Counts Too

Beyond rest breaks, other nonproductive time, time spent waiting for equipment, moving between fields, or handling tasks unrelated to the piece-rate activity, generally needs to be tracked and paid separately as well. If your day includes real chunks of time where you're not actively earning piece-rate pay but you're still under your employer's control, that time matters.

This often shows up at the start and end of a shift, loading equipment, walking to an assigned field, or waiting for a supervisor to assign the day's task. None of that is technically 'picking' or 'planting,' but all of it is still work time your employer controls and should be paying for.

05

How Overtime Works With Piece Rate

Overtime calculations for piece-rate workers get more complicated than a simple hourly rate, since your regular rate for overtime purposes has to account for your piece-rate earnings across the whole workweek, not just a flat number your employer picks. If overtime pay looks like it's calculated using a rate lower than what your piece-rate earnings would actually produce, that's worth double-checking.

Some employers simplify this by paying a flat, artificially low hourly rate for overtime hours instead of properly calculating your actual regular rate from your piece-rate earnings. That shortcut typically shortchanges the worker and doesn't satisfy California's overtime formula.

The general approach is to divide your total piece-rate earnings for the week by the total hours worked to find your regular rate, then apply the overtime premium to hours beyond 8 in a day or 40 in a week based on that number, not an arbitrary lower figure.

06

Common Violations We See

Common violations we see include no separate line item for rest break pay, piece-rate totals that fall under minimum wage with no employer top-up, and overtime calculated off an artificially low hourly rate that doesn't reflect real piece-rate earnings.

We also see pay stubs that lump everything together into one number, with no breakdown between piece-rate earnings, rest break pay, and any overtime premium. California requires that breakdown to be itemized clearly, and a pay stub that hides the math is a red flag on its own.

07

Reading Your Pay Stub Carefully

A compliant piece-rate pay stub should show your total units completed and the rate per unit, a separate line for paid rest break time, and, in weeks with overtime, a separate overtime calculation based on your actual regular rate. If your pay stub only shows one lump total with no breakdown at all, that alone is worth raising as a concern.

It's worth keeping copies of every pay stub you receive, even ones that look correct, since a pattern across several pay periods is often what reveals a problem that isn't obvious from any single week on its own.

08

Who This Affects Most

This affects fruit and vegetable pickers, packers, and pruning crews throughout California's agricultural regions, along with piece-rate mechanics and technicians in industries like auto repair, where similar piece-rate rules apply. Seasonal harvest workers moving between different growers throughout the year face this issue repeatedly, since piece-rate structures and how carefully they're tracked can vary a lot employer to employer.

Newer workers who haven't yet learned exactly how their specific employer calculates piece-rate pay are especially vulnerable, since it's hard to spot a shortfall in a system you don't fully understand yet. Asking directly how your rate per unit translates into an hourly equivalent is a reasonable question to ask, and a hesitant or vague answer is itself worth noting.

09

What to Do Next

Save your pay stubs alongside any personal count you keep of bins, rows, or trays completed each day, since that comparison is often the clearest way to spot a shortfall. This connects closely to the broader overtime rules we cover in our farm worker overtime guide.

Even a rough personal count kept on your phone or in a small notebook can be enough to establish a pattern worth investigating further. A free case review can look at your specific piece-rate structure and pay records.
