Bullied at Your Office Job? Can You Sue in California?

Micromanaging and exclusion are not always illegal, but harassment and retaliation can be.

TL;DR

California has no general anti-bullying law, so a harsh or unfair boss is not automatically breaking it. Office workers may be able to sue if the treatment is harassment based on a protected trait, retaliation for complaining or taking protected leave, or conditions that force them to quit. Office bullying is often quiet: public criticism in meetings, being left off emails, stolen credit, constant micromanaging, or a sudden performance plan after years of good reviews. It can feel like targeting, and many office workers ask whether they can sue. The answer is sometimes. Below, we explain what California law covers for office, remote and professional workers, and what to do.

1

Is Office Bullying Illegal in California?

Not by itself. California has no general law against bullying, so a micromanaging, rude or unfair boss is not automatically breaking the law. State law defines "abusive conduct" for employer training, but that does not create a lawsuit on its own.

It becomes a legal issue when the treatment is tied to a , to for speaking up or taking protected leave, or when it forms a .

A simple test helps: is the treatment connected to who you are, or to something you did that the law protects, such as complaining to HR, requesting an accommodation or taking protected leave? If so, you may have a claim even if the behavior looks like ordinary workplace toughness. If it is only a harsh personality with no link to a protected trait or protected activity, the law usually does not step in, which is why the details matter.

Example

An analyst returns from parental leave. Her manager, who praised her work for years, now excludes her from projects, criticizes her in meetings and puts her on a . The timing suggests the treatment may be linked to her protected leave.

2

What Office Bullying Looks Like

Common forms include public humiliation, constant criticism with no feedback, taking credit for your work, excluding you from meetings or channels, unreasonable deadlines and abusive emails or chats. These are frustrating, but they are usually unlawful only when connected to a protected trait or to retaliation.

Remote and hybrid work does not change the law. Harassment by video call, Slack or Teams counts the same as in person, and the messages are often useful evidence.

3

Performance Plans as a Setup

A performance improvement plan is not unlawful by itself. The warning sign is timing: a plan that appears right after you complained, requested an accommodation, announced a pregnancy, took leave or turned 40, especially when your past reviews were good.

If you are put on a plan, ask for the goals in writing, keep proof of your results, and respond professionally in writing if you disagree. Do not resign in anger before you speak with an attorney.

4

Retaliation and Protected Leave

Employees are protected when they complain about harassment or discrimination, report wage problems, or take protected leave for a serious health condition or to bond with a child. Punishing you for any of those can be unlawful retaliation.

If you were fired or pushed out after speaking up, read about complaining to HR and getting fired. If you are offered a package to leave, see what to check in a severance agreement before signing.

5

Protecting Yourself in an Office

Keep dated notes of what happened, who was there and how it affected your work. Save your performance reviews, praise emails and the messages that show the change. Use your own notes and device for personal records.

Be careful with company files. Do not forward confidential documents to a personal account without legal advice, and check your handbook. Report in writing to HR or a senior manager and keep a copy. Do not secretly record conversations, because California generally requires everyone's consent.

Do not answer a hostile email in anger or copy the whole team, because that can backfire. Keep replies short, factual and professional, and save the original messages. If you are tempted to quit on the spot, pause: resigning too fast can weaken a claim, and an attorney can help you weigh your options first.

6

Your Options and the Deadlines

You can use your employer's complaint process, file with the California Civil Rights Department (generally three years for harassment and discrimination), file a retaliation complaint with the Labor Commissioner, or bring a lawsuit. Federal complaints usually have shorter deadlines, often 300 days.
