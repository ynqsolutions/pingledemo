Meal Breaks on Film Sets in California: What Cast and Crew Should Know

A tight shooting schedule doesn't suspend California's meal break rules, it just makes violations more likely.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 28, 2026 · 7 min read

TL;DR

California generally requires a 30-minute unpaid meal break before the end of the fifth hour of work, and a second meal break once a shift passes 10 hours. Film sets are not exempt from this rule just because a scene is mid-setup or the light is about to go. A missed, late, or shortened meal break generally owes an extra hour of pay, and those premiums can add up fast across a whole crew.

Anyone who has worked a shoot day knows how easily a meal break slides. The company is losing light, the scene isn't in the can, and a production assistant is told to "grab something quick" between setups. None of that changes what California law requires. Meal and rest break rules apply to film and television production the same way they apply to a warehouse or a restaurant kitchen, and a packed schedule is never itself a legal excuse.

1

Do Film Sets Follow the Same Meal Break Rules?

Yes. Unless a specific role is properly classified as exempt, California's standard rules apply on a film set exactly as they would in any other industry. A 30-minute unpaid, duty-free meal break generally has to begin before the end of your fifth hour of work, regardless of whether the crew is behind schedule or mid-scene. "We'll break after this take" is a scheduling decision, not a legal exception, and it applies to background actors and day players the same as it does to full-time crew.

There's a persistent myth in production circles that entertainment work runs under its own separate labor rules, largely because the culture is built around long, unpredictable hours. That myth doesn't hold up legally, since wage orders apply based on the nature of the work and the employee's classification, not on which industry happens to be producing the paycheck.

Example

A one-day commercial shoot runs long, and the production tells everyone to "eat between setups." Even on a single-day job, that's still a meal period violation if no real 30-minute duty-free break happened before the fifth hour.

2

The Meal Penalty and Why It's So Common on Set

When a required meal break is missed, started late, cut short, or interrupted, California law generally owes the employee one additional hour of pay at their regular rate, and a separate hour for a missed rest break. Film sets see this constantly: a background actor held on standby through their fifth hour, or a grip called back early from lunch to reset a rig.

Example

A crew member has lunch cut short three separate days on a shoot to reset a rig early. That's three separate one-hour meal penalties owed, on top of regular wages for the day.

These premiums also stack up faster than most people expect because they're owed per workday, not per incident across a whole project, so a pattern of routinely rushed breaks can turn into a meaningful sum by the time a production wraps.

3

On-Duty Meal Periods: When They're Actually Allowed

Some crew positions genuinely can't be fully relieved of duty during a shift, and California law allows a narrow exception for that: an . The role has to make it truly impossible to be relieved, and both sides have to agree to it in writing. Critically, an on-duty meal is paid, not unpaid, and the employee can revoke that agreement at any time, which means a production can't simply assume the exception applies to an entire department by default.

Very few production roles actually meet this strict standard, even though the exception gets invoked casually across sets. A security guard alone at a remote gate might qualify, while a grip or camera assistant, roles where another crew member could reasonably step in for thirty minutes, typically does not.

4

Second Meal Breaks on Long Shoot Days

Once a shift runs past 10 hours, a second 30-minute meal break is generally required. That second break can sometimes be waived by mutual agreement if the first wasn't itself waived and the total shift stays at 12 hours or less, but past that point the second break generally has to happen. Skipping it on a 14-hour day is a separate violation on top of anything already owed for the first, and a valid waiver has to be a genuine choice, not a form signed as routine.

Long shoot days are exactly where this rule gets ignored most, since a crew pushing toward wrap is typically focused on finishing the shot list rather than tracking a second-break threshold. A production planning an aggressive 13 or 14-hour day without building in that second break is planning around a violation it should be able to see coming.

5

Who's Responsible: Production Company vs. Line Producer

Cast and crew are often employed by a production company formed specifically for that project or a payroll services company, while the line producer or 1st AD actually runs the schedule day to day. That layered structure doesn't erase liability, the entity that legally employs you can still be on the hook for meal and rest break violations regardless of who called "lunch" on set, and sometimes more than one entity shares responsibility.

Figuring out which entity actually bears responsibility for a specific violation is often a necessary first step before a wage claim can move forward, especially since a payroll services company may argue it simply processed the hours it was given rather than set the schedule itself. A joint employer above the production company can sometimes share that responsibility as well.

6

What to Do If Your Breaks Are Being Skipped

Keep your own informal log of when breaks actually happened versus when they were scheduled, since production paperwork doesn't always reflect what really occurred. Use our Meal & Rest Break Calculator to see how many breaks a given shift length should have included, and compare that against your actual day. If missed or shortened breaks are a repeated pattern, that pattern is worth having reviewed rather than treated as normal for the industry.

A simple note on your phone with the actual clock time you sat down to eat, and how long you got before being pulled back, is often more reliable evidence than production paperwork will show months later.
