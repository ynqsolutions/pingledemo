Hazed or Bullied on a Construction Site? Can You Sue in California?

Rough talk on a job site is not always illegal, but harassment, safety retaliation and threats can be.

Construction can be a rough environment. New workers and apprentices are sometimes hazed, women and workers of color may face slurs and jokes, and a foreman can threaten to drop you from the crew for speaking up. Many workers ask whether they can sue over it. The answer is sometimes. Below, we explain what California law covers on a job site and what to do.

1

Is Bullying Illegal for Construction Workers?

Not by itself. California has no general law against bullying, so a loud, demanding foreman or crew banter is not automatically breaking the law. State law defines "abusive conduct" for employer training, but that does not create a lawsuit on its own.

It becomes a legal issue when the treatment is tied to a , to for speaking up, or when it forms a .

A simple test helps: is the treatment connected to who you are, or to something you did that the law protects, such as raising a safety complaint, asking about overtime or reporting harassment? If so, you may have a claim even if the behavior looks like ordinary workplace toughness. If it is only a harsh personality with no link to a protected trait or protected activity, the law usually does not step in, which is why the details matter.

Example

A carpenter points out that scaffolding is unsafe and tells the superintendent he will call . The foreman calls him a snitch, drops him from the crew and tells others not to work with him. The treatment may be retaliation for his safety complaint.

2

Hazing, Slurs and Harassment on the Job Site

Harassment includes slurs, jokes about your race, language, religion or national origin, sexual comments or touching, and being singled out because of age, pregnancy or disability. Some crews call it hazing or joking around, but it is unlawful when it is tied to a protected trait and is severe or pervasive.

Women and workers of color in the trades often report that complaints go nowhere. The employer must take reasonable steps to prevent and correct harassment once it knows, including harassment by a subcontractor's crew.

3

Safety Complaints and Retaliation

You can complain about unsafe conditions to your employer or to Cal/OSHA without being punished. Being dropped from the crew, sent home, given dangerous tasks or blacklisted soon after a safety complaint can be unlawful retaliation.

Pay complaints are protected too. See our guides on overtime for construction workers and being paid in cash.

4

Threats About Immigration Status or Work

Some bullies threaten to call immigration authorities or to make sure you never work again when you speak up. California law generally protects workers regardless of immigration status, and threats made because you asserted your rights can be unlawful retaliation.

Save the message or write down the exact words, who said them and when, and speak with an attorney before you decide what to do.

5

Protecting Yourself on a Job Site

Keep dated notes of what happened, who saw it and how it affected your work. Save texts with the foreman, crew lists, timesheets and photos of hazards if it is safe to take them. Keep your own record of your hours and pay.

Report in writing to the contractor, owner or your union representative, and keep a copy. Do not secretly record conversations, because California generally requires everyone's consent.

Do not do work you believe is immediately dangerous just to avoid being dropped from the crew. If conditions are unsafe, report them in writing, and contact Cal/OSHA if the employer does not fix them. If you are unsure whether you can refuse a task, talk to your union representative or an attorney first, and keep a record of what you were asked to do.

6

Your Options and the Deadlines

You can use your employer's complaint process, file with the California Civil Rights Department (generally three years for harassment and discrimination), file a safety or retaliation complaint with Cal/OSHA or the Labor Commissioner, or bring a lawsuit. Federal complaints usually have shorter deadlines, often 300 days.
