How to Ask for a Raise at Work: What to Say and When to Ask

Asking for a raise isn't about luck or waiting to be noticed. Here's how to time it, what to say, and how to read the response.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 27, 2026 · 5 min read

TL;DR

Time your ask around a real accomplishment, not the calendar. Come with a specific number backed by data, ask directly, and treat the first answer as a starting point. Retaliating against you for simply asking, or for discussing pay with coworkers, is illegal in California.

Most people put off asking for a raise until they're frustrated enough to blurt it out in a review, which is close to the worst possible time to ask. A raise conversation goes better as a planned, specific request tied to your actual contribution, not a reaction to feeling underpaid.

Here's how to plan it instead of stumbling into it.

Share this article:
01

Why Timing Matters More Than You Think

The best moment to ask is right after you've delivered something measurable, closed a deal, shipped a project, absorbed a departed coworker's workload, not during a scheduled annual review where the budget may already be locked. Managers usually need lead time to get a raise approved, so asking a few weeks before budget cycles close gives your request somewhere to go.

It also helps to know your company's actual review and budget cycle rather than guessing. If raises are typically decided in a Q4 planning meeting, an ask made in November has almost nowhere to go until the next cycle, while the same ask made in September, before decisions are locked, can still influence the outcome. A quick, casual question to a manager or HR about when comp decisions generally happen isn't out of line and can save you months of bad timing.

Example

You just took over a second territory after a coworker left. That's a concrete, recent reason, not a vague sense that you deserve more.

02

What to Actually Say

Ask directly instead of hinting. State what you've taken on, name a specific number or range, and pause. Something like: "Over the past six months I've taken on X and delivered Y. Based on that, I'd like to discuss moving my salary to $Z." Silence after the ask is normal and useful, resist the urge to fill it by negotiating against yourself.

Schedule the conversation deliberately rather than catching your manager off guard in a hallway or at the end of an unrelated meeting. A short, calendared 15-minute conversation signals that the topic is serious and gives your manager a chance to prepare, rather than react on the spot with whatever answer is easiest in the moment.

Example

"I was hoping for a raise sometime" invites no response. "I'd like to move to $85,000 based on the accounts I've brought in this year" invites a decision.

03

How Much to Ask For, and How to Back It Up

A number that's clearly researched lands differently than a round figure pulled from nowhere. Look at market pay data for your role and region, document specific outcomes you've driven, and if you know or reasonably suspect that coworkers doing substantially similar work are paid more, that's worth raising directly, California law protects your right to . Employers are also legally required to provide the pay scale for a position upon request under Labor Code Section 432.3, which can be useful context before you name a number.

Beyond market data, keep a running log of your own accomplishments throughout the year rather than trying to reconstruct them right before the conversation. Specific numbers, projects completed, revenue influenced, processes improved, hold up far better than a general sense that you've "been doing a lot lately." The more concrete the evidence, the harder it is for the request to be dismissed as just a feeling.

04

What to Do If the Answer Is No

A "not right now" is not necessarily a dead end. Ask what specifically would need to be true for the answer to change, and get a rough timeline in writing, even if it's just a follow-up email summarizing the conversation. If budget is the stated reason, non-salary asks like additional PTO, a title change, remote flexibility, or a defined path to the next review can still move even when a raise itself is frozen.

If you do get a specific commitment for a future review, put it in writing yourself if the employer doesn't. A short recap email, "Thanks for the conversation, to confirm, we'll revisit this in Q2 once the new budget is approved," creates a record that's hard to walk back later and costs nothing to send.

05

When a "No" Might Be Something Else

Most declined raise requests are just that, declined, for ordinary business reasons. But a pattern is worth paying attention to: if you're consistently paid less than colleagues doing substantially similar work, and the gap tracks along gender, race, or another protected characteristic, that can raise questions under California's . And if a raise request or a pay discussion is followed by a sudden shift in how you're treated, reduced hours, a bad review out of nowhere, or termination, that timing itself can matter. Our guide to pay equity and misclassification in California covers this in more depth, and the Equal Pay Act glossary entry breaks down the legal standard in plain terms.

The same negotiation instincts apply later too. If you ever find yourself negotiating a severance offer instead of a raise, the underlying skill, coming with a number and a reason, doesn't change. Our guide to negotiating a low severance offer walks through that version of the conversation.

If you do suspect a pay gap tied to a protected characteristic, resist the urge to confront a coworker directly about their specific salary before you have a broader sense of the pattern. A single data point can be a coincidence, driven by tenure, prior experience, or a different negotiated starting offer. It's the pattern across multiple similarly situated employees that tends to matter most, which is exactly the kind of thing worth having reviewed rather than acted on alone.
