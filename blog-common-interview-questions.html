Common Interview Questions and How to Answer Them

The 12 questions you are most likely to be asked, what the employer is really asking, and a simple way to answer each one.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

The most common job interview questions are: tell me about yourself, why do you want this job, why should we hire you, what are your strengths and weaknesses, tell me about a conflict or mistake, why are you leaving your job, where do you see yourself in five years, how do you handle pressure, what are your salary expectations, and do you have any questions for us. For each one, keep your answer specific, short and tied to the job.

Most interviews repeat the same handful of questions. Preparing a short, honest answer to each one makes you calmer and clearer on the day. Below is each common question, what the interviewer is really trying to learn, and how to answer it. Use practice mode above to test yourself: say your answer out loud first, then reveal the sample.

A simple way to answer almost any question

For questions about past experience, use STAR: Situation (the context in a sentence), Task (what you needed to do), Action (what you did) and Result (what happened). Keep most of the answer on the action and the result, and aim for about one to two minutes.

The 12 most common interview questions

Tell me about yourself.

What they're really asking: They want a short, relevant summary, not your life story.

How to answer: Give a 30 to 60 second answer in three parts: what you do now, one or two things you are proud of, and why this role is the next step. For example: "I have spent five years in customer service, most recently leading a team of six. I'm proud of cutting our complaint rate by a third. I'm looking for a role where I can keep solving customer problems and take on more responsibility."

Why do you want this job?

What they're really asking: They are checking that you understand the role and chose it on purpose.

How to answer: Name one or two specific things about the job or company that appeal to you, and connect them to what you do well. Avoid answers that are only about pay, location or convenience.

Why should we hire you?

What they're really asking: They want to know what you bring that others may not.

How to answer: Match your strongest skills to the main needs in the job posting. Pick two or three, give a quick example of each, and finish with what you would help them achieve.

What are your greatest strengths?

What they're really asking: They want strengths that fit this job, backed by proof.

How to answer: Choose strengths the job actually needs, and support each with a short example. "I'm dependable" is stronger as "I haven't missed a shift in two years and trained three new hires."

What is your biggest weakness?

What they're really asking: They want self-awareness and evidence that you work on it.

How to answer: Pick a real, manageable weakness that is not central to the job, then explain what you are doing about it. For example: "I used to take on too much myself. I now use a weekly task list and ask for help earlier."

Tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation or conflict at work.

What they're really asking: They are looking at how you behave under pressure and with other people.

How to answer: Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Spend most of the answer on what you did and how it turned out. Stay fair and calm about the other person, and focus on the solution.

Why did you leave your last job, or why are you leaving?

What they're really asking: They want a clear, professional reason and to see how you talk about past employers.

How to answer: Keep it short, truthful and forward-looking: you were looking for growth, a better fit, more responsibility or a different schedule. Avoid criticizing a former employer, and move quickly to why this role appeals to you.

Where do you see yourself in five years?

What they're really asking: They want to know whether the role fits your goals and whether you plan to grow.

How to answer: Describe the direction you want to grow in, not a specific title. For example: "I'd like to build deeper skills in this field and take on more responsibility, ideally in a role like this one."

How do you handle pressure or tight deadlines?

What they're really asking: They want to know whether you stay organized when things get busy.

How to answer: Describe your method, such as prioritizing, breaking work into steps and communicating early if something slips, then give one real example with the result.

Tell me about a time you made a mistake.

What they're really asking: They want honesty, ownership and what you learned.

How to answer: Pick a real mistake with a limited impact. Say what happened, what you did to fix it, and what you changed so it would not happen again. Do not blame others.

What are your salary expectations?

What they're really asking: They want to confirm your expectations are in line with the budget.

How to answer: Research the typical range for the role and location first. Give a range based on that research rather than a single number, and say you are open to discussing the full package. If you are unsure, it is fine to ask what range they have in mind.

Do you have any questions for us?

What they're really asking: They are checking your interest and how seriously you have thought about the role.

How to answer: Always have two or three. Good ones include: What does success look like in the first 90 days? What is the team like? What are the next steps in the process? Avoid asking only about pay or time off at this stage.

Adjusting your answers for your job

The questions stay the same. What changes is the proof you use:

  • Retail and customer service: use examples of calm, friendly problem-solving with a customer.
  • Warehouse and driving: use dependability, safety habits, accuracy and meeting targets.
  • Office and administrative: use organization, accuracy, software skills and handling competing deadlines.
  • Healthcare and caregiving: use examples of patience, teamwork and following procedures under pressure.
  • Restaurant and hospitality: use teamwork in a rush, handling guests and reliability with your schedule.
