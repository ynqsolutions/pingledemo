Does a Hotel Have to Pay Housekeepers for Time Spent Preparing Carts and Supplies?

Almost always, yes. Cart stocking and supply runs are work, and work has to be paid.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR

Time spent stocking a housekeeping cart, gathering linens, or picking up supplies before the first room is generally under California law, even if it happens before the shift technically starts on paper. Whether a hotel exercises enough control over that time is usually the deciding question.

Before a housekeeper ever touches a room, there's often ten, fifteen, sometimes twenty minutes of prep: pulling linens, restocking amenities, loading a cart. Hotels across Fountain Valley, San Bernardino, and Sacramento frequently treat this time as informal, unpaid warm-up. California law generally disagrees.

01

The Control Test

California uses what's often called the "control test" to decide whether time counts as compensable work: if the employer exercises control over how you spend that time, requiring you to be at a specific place, doing a specific task, on a specific timeline, it's generally paid time, regardless of whether you've officially clocked in yet.

The control test doesn't require the employer to have handed you a written policy. Verbal instructions from a supervisor, an unwritten but consistently enforced expectation, or even a schedule that only works if you show up early all count as employer control. What matters is whether a reasonable employee in your position would feel they had no real choice but to start early, not whether a handbook technically says so.

Example

A housekeeper is scheduled to clock in at 9:00 a.m., but the supervisor expects carts fully stocked and rolling by 9:00 so the floor can start on time. To meet that expectation, she arrives at 8:40 each day to pull linens and load her cart before clocking in. Because the hotel effectively requires that head start to hit its own timeline, that twenty minutes is generally compensable, even though nobody wrote it into a policy.

02

What Morillion v. Royal Packing Established

The California Supreme Court's 2000 decision in Morillion v. Royal Packing Co. is the leading case on this point. Agricultural workers were required to ride employer-provided buses to remote job sites and weren't paid for that travel time. The court held that because the employer required and controlled that travel, it was compensable work time, even though the workers weren't yet doing their actual farm labor.

The same logic applies directly to a housekeeper required to arrive early, check in at a specific supply closet, and build a cart before starting on rooms. If the hotel requires it and controls how it's done, it's paid time.

Morillion also drew a useful line: time isn't automatically compensable just because it happens on employer property or benefits the employer in some general sense. The key question is control, not location or convenience. A housekeeper who chooses, entirely on her own, to arrive early to grab coffee before her shift isn't doing compensable work. A housekeeper who arrives early because supply carts must be built at a fixed location before floor assignments go out is a different situation entirely, because the hotel is dictating the task and the timeline, not just benefiting from an employee's personal choice.

03

Bag Checks and the Frlekin v. Apple Case

A more recent case, Frlekin v. Apple Inc. (2020), reinforced this same control-based approach in a retail setting involving mandatory bag checks. The California Supreme Court held that because Apple required the checks and employees couldn't leave the premises without completing them, the time was compensable, regardless of how long the actual search took. The same reasoning extends to a housekeeper required to complete cart prep, sign out keys, or restock supplies before beginning room assignments, since the hotel is directing exactly what happens during that window.

One detail from Frlekin matters a lot for prep-time claims: the court rejected the idea that the time has to be substantial to count. Even a few minutes, if required and controlled, adds up over a pay period and is owed. Employers sometimes argue that cart prep "only takes a couple minutes" as if that makes it not worth paying for. California law doesn't recognize a minimum-time exception like that; if it's required work, it gets paid, whether it's two minutes or twenty.

04

Who This Affects Most

Housekeepers at large resort properties in Beverly Hills and San Diego, where linen closets and supply rooms are often centralized and require travel time before reaching an assigned floor, tend to lose the most unpaid prep time. Housekeepers at extended-stay hotels across San Bernardino and Fountain Valley, where amenity restocking is done individually rather than by a separate supply team, often face similar unpaid setup routines. And housekeepers at unionized convention hotels in San Francisco and Sacramento sometimes have this issue addressed in a collective bargaining agreement, which is worth checking closely if one exists.

Seasonal and event-driven staffing changes this picture too. During peak occupancy weeks, resort properties often add extra housekeepers who are newer, less familiar with informal routines, and less likely to question a supervisor's instructions about arriving early. That combination of temporary staff and heavy workload is exactly when off-the-clock prep time is most likely to slip through unpaid, and it's worth extra attention if you were hired seasonally or through a staffing agency.

05

Common Violations

Watch for a required check-in time at a supply closet that's earlier than the official clock-in time, mandatory cart-building before rooms can be assigned, and any expectation that supplies be gathered "before the shift starts" without that time being logged.

Another common pattern: a time clock that only lets employees punch in once they reach the floor or a specific terminal, so the walk to the supply room and cart-building happen before the system even records a start time. If the timekeeping system's design itself pushes required work earlier than the recorded clock-in, that's a red flag worth raising, since the paper record won't show the unpaid time even though it happened.

06

What to Do Next

Track the actual time between when you arrive and start prepping versus when you officially clock in. This connects to our guide on finishing rooms after clocking out, the same off-the-clock work issue from the other end of the shift. A free case review can look at your specific hotel's prep routine, wherever in California you work.

If possible, keep your own contemporaneous notes: the time you actually arrive, the time you actually start prep, and the time you clock in, ideally with a photo timestamp on your phone or a text to yourself. Employers are required to keep accurate time records, but when those records don't reflect reality, your own notes can be the difference between a claim that goes nowhere and one that's easy to document. California generally allows unpaid wage claims to reach back several years, so even if this has been going on for a while, it's usually still worth raising.
