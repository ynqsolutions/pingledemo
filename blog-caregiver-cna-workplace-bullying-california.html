Bullied as a Caregiver or CNA? Can You Sue in California?

Rough treatment from supervisors, nurses or families is not always illegal, but harassment and retaliation can be.

Caregivers and certified nursing assistants do hard, physical work for low pay, often with too few staff. Some are yelled at by nurses or supervisors, blamed for things outside their control, or treated roughly by residents' families. Many ask whether they can sue. The answer is sometimes. Below, we explain what California law covers for CNAs, home health aides and other care workers, and what to do.

1

Is Bullying Illegal for CNAs and Caregivers?

Not by itself. California has no general law against bullying, so a rude or demanding supervisor, nurse or coworker is not automatically breaking the law. State law defines "abusive conduct" for employer training, but that does not create a lawsuit on its own.

It becomes a legal issue when the treatment is tied to a , to for speaking up, or when it is severe or pervasive enough to create a .

A simple test helps: is the treatment connected to who you are, or to something you did that the law protects, such as reporting abuse or neglect, asking about overtime or taking protected leave? If so, you may have a claim even if the behavior looks like ordinary workplace toughness. If it is only a harsh personality with no link to a protected trait or protected activity, the law usually does not step in, which is why the details matter.

Example

A CNA at a nursing home reports that residents are going unchanged because of short staffing. Her supervisor stops giving her preferred shifts, writes her up for small mistakes and tells coworkers not to talk to her. The treatment may be retaliation for her report.

2

Harassment by Supervisors, Coworkers and Residents

Harassment includes unwelcome conduct tied to race, sex, age, religion, pregnancy, disability or national origin: mocking your accent, comments about your body, or pressure for dates or favors. In care work it may also come from residents, clients or family members.

Behavior linked to a resident's illness, such as confusion, is different from a family member or aide targeting you for who you are. Either way, your employer must take reasonable steps to protect you once it knows, and not simply tell you to put up with it.

3

Retaliation for Reporting Abuse, Neglect or Understaffing

Many care workers are s. If you report suspected abuse or neglect, or complain about unsafe staffing or missed breaks, California law protects you from punishment for doing so. Sudden schedule cuts, write-ups, reassignment or being frozen out after a report can support a retaliation claim.

Pay problems are protected too. Learn more about overtime for CNAs and overtime for in-home caregivers.

4

Threats to Your Certification or Your Job

Some bullies threaten to report a CNA to the state or to call the agency. A report about real misconduct is allowed, but threats or reports made to punish you for complaining can be part of a retaliation claim.

In-home caregivers are often employed by an agency or directly by a family. Either one can be your employer, and both have duties under the law. If you are unsure who your employer is, ask an attorney.

5

Protecting Yourself on the Job

Write down what happens: date, time, place, exact words and who saw it. Save schedules, staffing sheets, texts and write-ups. Do not copy resident records or write down residents' private health details. Describe the event without identifying the resident.

Report in writing to your supervisor, director of nursing or HR, and keep a copy. If you are in a union, tell your representative. Do not secretly record conversations, because California generally requires everyone's consent.

Do not leave residents unattended or walk off a shift in frustration, because that can create a separate problem for your certification and your job. Keep caring for residents professionally while you document what is happening, and avoid venting in group chats or on social media, where your words can be used against you.

6

Your Options and the Deadlines

You can use your employer's complaint process, file with the California Civil Rights Department (generally three years for harassment and discrimination), file a retaliation complaint with the Labor Commissioner (often one year), or bring a lawsuit. Federal complaints usually have shorter deadlines, often 300 days.
