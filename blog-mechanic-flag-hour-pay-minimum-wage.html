I’m a Mechanic Paid by the Job or Flag Hour. Am I Still Owed Minimum Wage and Overtime?

Yes. Piece-rate and flag-hour pay doesn't exempt a shop from California's wage floor.

Free Consultation Contact Us
By Corey A. Pingle, Esq. Employment Law Attorney

August 26, 2026 · 4 min read

TL;DR

Technicians paid by the job are still owed at least minimum wage for every hour worked, plus separate pay for non-productive time like waiting on parts or attending meetings.

If you're paid by the job, flag hour, or commission at a shop, you're still protected by the same baseline wage laws as everyone else. California courts have been pretty clear on this, and a lot of shops still get it wrong.

Share this article:
01

Piece-Rate Pay and the Minimum Wage Floor

California requires that piece-rate and flag-hour pay average out to at least minimum wage for every hour actually worked during a pay period, not just the hours that got flagged.

Example

A slow week where flagged hours only add up to $400, but you were on the clock for 40 hours, may fall short of the wage floor.

02

Non-Productive Time Must Be Paid Separately

Time spent not actively working on a flagged job, cleaning, waiting for parts, mandatory training, has to be paid separately at least at minimum wage, on top of your flag-hour earnings.

Example

A mandatory Monday morning meeting with no job attached to bill it to.

03

Reimbursement for Your Own Tools

Most shops require technicians to supply their own tool set, and that requirement carries a legal cost the shop is supposed to cover. Under and California's wage orders, employers generally must reimburse technicians earning less than twice the applicable minimum wage for the tools and equipment the job requires.

This is separate from, and stacks on top of, any flag-hour or overtime shortfall. If your shop never covered your tool costs, or only offered a token allowance, that's worth raising alongside a wage claim, not instead of one.

Example

Your shop expects a full tool box on day one and never reimburses a cent, while your flag-hour pay barely clears minimum wage.

04

Overtime Still Applies

If your total hours worked exceed 8 in a day or 40 in a week, you're still owed overtime, calculated using your regular rate, not just your flagged pay.

Example

A 50-hour week at a shop that only calculates pay based on flagged jobs, with no separate overtime line.

05

How to Check Your Own Numbers

Compare your total hours on-site to your total pay for the period. If it doesn't clear minimum wage, or overtime isn't reflected, that gap is worth having reviewed.

Wage shortfalls, missing overtime, and uncovered tool costs are all part of the same unpaid wages and overtime claim, so it's usually worth having your entire pay structure looked at together rather than piece by piece.

06

Paying a Helper Out of Your Own Pocket

Some shops lean on an informal arrangement where a flat-rate or flag-hour technician pays a helper or apprentice directly, out of their own earnings, to assist with heavy jobs, parts runs, or cleanup between rows. It's common across shops in San Bernardino, Fountain Valley, and Beverly Hills, and it's legally shakier than most technicians realize.

Under California law, that helper is doing work that benefits the shop, often under the shop's roof and direction, which generally makes them the shop's employee, not the technician's, regardless of who physically hands over the cash. A shop can't outsource its own minimum wage, payroll tax, and workers' compensation obligations onto a technician who has no authority to actually hire anyone. If that helper gets hurt on the job, the shop's workers' comp coverage should apply, but an off-books arrangement can make that coverage murky exactly when it matters most.

There's also a wage problem for the technician. If a shop expects flat-rate pay to silently cover the cost of a helper the shop itself should be staffing and paying, that's functionally shifting a business expense onto the employee, the same kind of problem Labor Code Section 2802 is meant to prevent for tools and equipment. A technician shouldn't have to subsidize the shop's staffing decisions out of pay that's supposed to be theirs.

07

Who This Affects Most

Flag-hour and piece-rate pay problems are most common at dealership service departments in Beverly Hills and San Diego, and independent and franchise repair shops across San Bernardino, Fountain Valley, and Sacramento. Techs early in their careers, still building speed, are hit hardest, since flag-hour pay assumes a level of efficiency that takes years to reach.
